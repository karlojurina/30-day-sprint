How to Actually Submit Your First Ad Bounty

This isn't a technical guide. You'll figure out the technical stuff from the system itself and from the Discord channels. This is the tactical guide. The moves that actually decide whether your ad performs or flops.

There are 4 of them.

01 Choose your brand

If you've completed the 30-day sprint, you should already be inside the Ad Bounty program. If you're not, apply here: adbounty.netlify.app. Takes 5 minutes. Within 24 to 48 hours you're approved.

The first real decision happens here. Which brand do you work on?

There are 5 active brands on the program right now. Look through all of them. Read each brief. Spend 10 minutes scrolling each brand's existing ads.

Pick the one you're naturally drawn to. The one you think you could do your best work for. The one whose audience you instinctively get.

The filter behind that gut feeling is push vs pull motivation. One of the most underrated concepts in all of work.

Push motivation is when you have to force yourself to do the thing. You can do it. The skill is there. But every hour requires willpower. You're negotiating with yourself the whole time. By hour two you're tired. By hour three you're looking for excuses to step away. Push work drains you faster than the work itself ever should.

Pull motivation is the opposite. The work pulls you toward it. The clock disappears. Three hours of research feels like 20 minutes. You think about the project in the shower, on a walk, when you're trying to fall asleep. Not because you're disciplined. Because you're actually curious about figuring it out. The work is doing half the lifting for you, and you're getting better with every hour because you're fully present.

This shows up in every part of life. The career you have to talk yourself into every Monday vs the career that has you up at 1 AM still working because you forgot to eat. The hobby you should do vs the one you actually do. The friend you have to call back vs the friend you can't wait to see.

The same pattern decides which students hit a winning ad in 3 weeks and which students burn out in 3 weeks and never come back.

If you pick a brand with push, you can grind through one bounty. Maybe two. By the third one you'll be done. The work won't compound because you weren't really in it.

If you pick a brand with pull, you'll naturally put in more reps. You'll know the audience better. You'll iterate faster. You'll spend extra time studying the ads because you actually want to. The math from earlier in this article quietly starts compounding in your favor.

The brand with pull is the brand you're going to crush.

The brand with push is the brand you'll quit halfway through the brief.

Two more moves before you commit:

  • Go to the bounty chat in Discord and ask which brands students are submitting for right now. You'll learn which brands are taking new ads aggressively, and you'll find the students already doing the work.
  • Ask the coaches for tips. They've seen hundreds of bounties across all 5 brands. They know which angles are winning, which brands are easier to break into for first-timers, which ones are saturated.

Then enroll. The bot DMs you a unique submission link for that brand. Pin that message and don't lose it.

02 Study the brief like you mean it

Spend 1 to 2 hours on the brief. Minimum.

This is the move that separates winning ads from random shots in the dark. Most students rush through it. You won't.

Here's the order:

  • Read the entire brief end to end. Twice.
  • Open the brand's ad library and watch every single ad they're currently running. Note which angles repeat. Note which ones you'd stop scrolling for.
  • Read the market research document the brief provides. Read it slowly. Highlight the language the audience uses to describe their problem.
  • Go through all the content and assets the brief provides (images, B-roll, fonts, brand guidelines). Download what you need into a folder on your computer named after the brand.
  • Then go deeper than the brief. Read Reddit threads where the audience talks about the product category. Read Amazon reviews of similar products (the 3-star reviews are where the gold is). Find TikTok videos in the niche and read the comments.
  • Build your own market research document for the brand. Customer beliefs, desires, pain points, language, awareness stages. Same framework taught in the creative strategy section of the program. Make it yours.
  • Go into the brand-specific Discord channel. Read what students have been asking. Read what the brand owner has been answering. Many brand owners are active in their channel. If you have a question about their product, ask them directly. You'll get a real answer from the person whose money is on the line.

By the end of this, you should know the brand better than half their existing customers do.

That's when you're ready for Move 3.

04 Generate ideas (and validate them)

By the time you've done the research from Move 2 and set up your Claude project from Move 3, you should already have a couple of ideas floating in your head.

That's not an accident. That's how this works. The more time you spend studying a brand, studying their audience, studying their existing ads, the more ideas surface on their own. Three hours of focused research will generate more ideas than three weeks of staring at a blank page trying to force one.

When the ideas come, capture them. Write them down in your Growth Guide, in your Claude project, in a note app, in a voice memo. Wherever. Don't trust your memory. You will forget.

If the ideas aren't flowing, you didn't spend enough time on the brief. Go back to Move 2.

Aim for 3 to 4 strong angles. Not 1 perfect one. Not 20 mediocre ones. 3 to 4 you'd actually be proud to ship.

The Five Scenarios After Your Ad Goes Live

The moment your ad gets approved and launched, you get access to a personalized dashboard for that brand.

It looks the same as the one in Elijah's screenshots earlier. Your name. Your Creator ID. The brand's logo. And live performance metrics for every single ad of yours running in their account. Spend. Revenue. Purchases. ROAS. Cost per purchase. All of it updates as the day moves.

That dashboard is where you live now.

Open it once a day. Sometimes twice. Don't obsess over it minute by minute, but check in. Watch the numbers move. Build the muscle of looking at performance data and understanding what it's telling you.

Every ad you put up will land in one of five scenarios. But before we walk through them, there's some context that applies to all five.

First, the context

1. What KPI means.

Every brand has a KPI (key performance indicator) for their ads. Usually it's ROAS (return on ad spend), which is the multiple of revenue the brand needs to generate for every dollar of ad spend to stay profitable.

If a brand needs 2.5x ROAS to be profitable on their margins, their KPI is 2.5x. If your ad is doing 1.5x, it's "missing KPI" and the brand is losing money on every dollar the ad spends. If your ad is doing 3.5x, it's "hitting KPI" and the brand is making money on your ad, which means they'll keep it running.

You'll see "missing KPI" and "hitting KPI" used throughout the rest of this section. That's what they mean.

2. The brand doesn't decide what gets spent on your ad. Meta does.

Almost every brand on the Ad Bounty program runs ads through what's called a CBO campaign. CBO stands for Campaign Budget Optimization.

Here's how it works. The brand sets a daily budget for the whole campaign. Let's say $10,000 a day. Then they put all of their active ads inside that campaign. Yours, every other student's, every ad their internal team made. Meta's algorithm decides every single day which of those ads gets the budget.

If your ad is performing, Meta pushes more budget to it. If it's not, Meta starves it. Every ad in the campaign is competing against every other ad for the same daily pool of money.

Two things to take from this.

First: nobody on the brand's team is sitting there deciding "we like your ad, we'll give it $5K today." There's no human in the loop. There's no favoritism. It's just Meta watching how the audience reacts and pushing budget to whatever is working.

Second: this is why your only job is to make a good ad. If it's good, Meta will spend on it. If it's not, Meta won't. The system is honest like that.

3. Ad spend is unpredictable.

Sometimes your ad sits at $5 of spend for 5 days, then explodes on day 6 and starts ripping. Sometimes it spends $3K on day 1 and dies completely by day 3. Sometimes it spends slowly for two weeks before it takes off. Sometimes nothing happens at all.

The dashboard is going to show you weird stuff. Don't draw conclusions too fast. Watch the trend, not the moment.

OK. With that out of the way, here are the five scenarios you'll see on your dashboard:

  • Scenario 1: Your ad isn't getting spend.
  • Scenario 2: Your ad is hitting KPI but not getting enough spend.
  • Scenario 3: Your ad is spending but missing KPI.
  • Scenario 4: Your ad is spending well, then dies.
  • Scenario 5: Your ad is spending, hitting KPI, and scaling.

Let me walk through each one and tell you exactly what to do.

01 Your ad isn't getting spend

You open the dashboard. Your ad is live. But the spend column is showing $1. $3. $7. Almost nothing. The numbers aren't moving.

Here's what's actually happening underneath the dashboard.

Meta's algorithm tested your ad against a small group of viewers. Maybe 100 people. Maybe a few hundred. Those people scrolled past it without engaging. They didn't watch. They didn't click. They didn't react. Based on that small test, Meta made a decision: this ad isn't relevant enough to keep showing. And it stopped pushing budget toward it.

When this happens, there's really only one question to ask yourself.

Why does nobody care about this ad?

Pull it up. Watch the first 4 seconds the way a stranger would. Forget that you made it. Forget how many hours you put into the editing. Imagine you're scrolling through Instagram on your couch and this ad shows up in your feed. Would you stop?

If you wouldn't stop, the algorithm wouldn't either.

The fix is almost always in the first 4 seconds. Either your hook is too vague, or your visuals aren't calling out who this ad is for, or you're not giving Meta enough signal about who the perfect audience for it should be. Meta doesn't know who to show this to. So it shows it to nobody specific. And nobody specific cares.

So how do you fix it.

Make the first 1 second of the ad visually scream who it's for. A specific problem. A specific person. A specific moment a real human would recognize from their own life. If your ad is for women in their 30s dealing with hair thinning, the opening shot should make that exact woman feel "this is for me" before any words are even spoken. If your ad is for runners with knee pain, the opening shot should make any runner with knee pain feel that same recognition.

Then make the first line of copy or voiceover sharpen the signal even more. Say the problem out loud. Name the person. Make it impossible to misread who this ad is talking to.

The goal of the first 4 seconds is two things, and only these two things. Impossible to scroll past. Impossible to misread who this is for.

Re-shoot the opening. Submit it as a new variation. Try again.

One more thing worth saying. Sometimes ads just never take off, no matter what you do. You think it's the best ad you've ever made, Meta gives it $5 over the course of two weeks, and that's that. When this happens, don't agonize over it. Take whatever you can learn from the lack of response and ship the next bounty. Your first few ads in this program are about getting reps in, not about perfection.

02 Your ad is hitting KPI but not getting enough spend

Your dashboard shows your ad has been getting some spend each day. Not a lot, but consistently spending something. The numbers are moving, just at a small level.

Technically, when an ad is performing well, the spend should keep growing day over day. Meta should keep pushing more and more budget to it. But yours isn't. It stays at the same low level. Day after day, week after week.

The ROAS column tells you why this one is so frustrating. It's good. Whatever Meta did push to your ad, your ad converted. The KPI is being hit. The ad is technically working.

The problem: Meta won't scale it.

This is a really common scenario, and it's a frustrating one. Your ad is working. The few people who saw it bought. So why is Meta starving the budget?

Three things are usually happening underneath.

1. The hook isn't strong enough to stop more people. The hook is working on some viewers (enough that the small group Meta tested converted), but it's not strong enough to make a broader audience stop scrolling. Meta only pushes more spend to ads where the early stop-scroll signal is strong. Your ad is converting the people who do stop, but not enough of them are stopping in the first place.

2. The audience signal in the ad is too narrow. Meta uses your ad's visuals, copy, and audio to figure out who in the world to show it to. If your ad's signals point to a very specific slice of humanity (say, "big dog owners in Australia who run marathons"), Meta can't find enough of them to keep scaling. The ad converts inside that slice, but the slice is too small.

3. The bridge from hook to body is broken. People watch the first 3 seconds. They stop scrolling. The hook worked. But between the hook and the rest of the ad, they drop off. The hook stopped them, but the next 5 to 10 seconds didn't hold them. Meta sees the pattern (many people stopping, few watching through) and decides the ad isn't worth more budget.

What you do:

If the hook seems weak (Meta is barely pushing any spend at all), rewrite the hook. Make it more visually striking, more curiosity-spiking, more impossible-to-misread who it's for.

If the audience signal is too narrow, broaden the angle. Take your specific avatar and rewrite it for a slightly broader version of them. The angle that works for "big dog owners in Australia" might also work for "big dog owners" everywhere if you widen the framing just a bit.

If the bridge is broken, the fix is in seconds 4 to 10 of the ad. The transition between your hook and your main body has to feel seamless. The viewer needs an immediate reason to keep watching after the hook lands.

This scenario is actually a great place to be. You've already proven the ad can convert. You just need to get more eyeballs on it. Iterate, resubmit, and watch the spend start to scale.

03 Your ad is spending but missing KPI

Your ad is getting real spend. The dashboard is filling in, numbers are climbing day over day. Meta is pushing budget to your ad aggressively.

But the ROAS is below target. The brand is spending more on your ad than the ad is generating in revenue. You're winning attention but losing the sale.

This is not a reach problem. This is a belief problem.

People see your ad. The hook worked. They stopped scrolling. Meta found the audience. But they're not pulling out their wallet. Which means somewhere between the first second of the ad and the checkout page, you lost them.

Three things most often cause this:

Your claims aren't landing. The ad makes a promise, but it doesn't make the viewer believe the promise is real. They watched. They thought "nice ad." They didn't buy because they didn't trust it would actually work for them.

The ad-to-landing-page gap. Your ad pitches one angle. The landing page they click into pitches a different one. The viewer feels the disconnect, gets confused, and leaves.

You opened loops you didn't close. Your hook raised a question or promised an answer that the rest of the ad didn't fully deliver. Viewers were intrigued enough to watch through, but at the end they felt unsatisfied. They had questions you didn't answer. So they kept scrolling instead of buying.

What you do:

Add proof. Real customer results. Real before/after. Real testimonials. Real screenshots of reviews. Anything that takes the promise from claim to evidence.

Then study the landing page. Click your ad. Land where they land. Read what they read. Does the page hand the viewer the same emotional moment your ad set up? Or does it pivot to a different sales angle?

If your ad opened questions or curiosity loops, make sure the body of the ad actually closes them. The viewer should hit your CTA feeling like every question they had is answered. Not curious. Not hanging. Resolved.

And one more thing most beginners miss. Most ads that miss KPI also miss urgency. The viewer liked your ad, but they didn't feel they needed to buy right now. Build in real stakes. What does the viewer lose by not solving this problem? What does waiting actually cost them? You're not creating fake urgency. You're surfacing the real cost of not buying.

The fix here isn't more attention. It's belief plus urgency. Make whatever your ad is promising feel actually achievable for the person watching, and make it feel like buying today matters more than buying next month.

04 Your ad is spending well, then dies

This one is sneaky.

For a few days, your ad is printing. Spend is up. ROAS is healthy. Purchases are coming in. You're checking the dashboard every two hours. You're celebrating.

Then it just... stops.

The spend drops. The purchases stop. The ROAS falls off a cliff. Within a week the brand has rotated your ad out of active testing.

Here's what happened.

You wrote a bottom-of-funnel ad. It worked for the warm audience. People already aware of the problem your product solves. People already shopping for solutions. People who've maybe seen the brand before. They converted easily.

But Meta is always expanding the audience. Once your ad burned through the warm pool, Meta opened it up to colder traffic. People who don't know they have the problem. People who've never heard of this product category. People who have to be educated, not just sold to.

Your bottom-of-funnel pitch didn't translate. Cold audiences don't buy logical pitches. They buy emotional ones.

What you do:

This is actually a great problem to have. A dying ad means you made something that worked for someone, and you're closer to a winning ad than you think. You don't have to start from scratch. You just have to take the same angle and rewrite it for a different awareness stage.

The fix is to move the ad up the market awareness ladder. Instead of pitching the product directly, you start by surfacing the problem in a way that makes the viewer feel it before they even know they're being sold to. You make them recognize themselves in the ad. You make them feel the pain of the problem before you ever mention there's a solution waiting for them.

If you can make a cold viewer feel a sharp pang of "wait, that's me" in the first 3 seconds, you've done the hard part. Now they're paying attention. Now they want to know what comes next. Now they're open to hearing about the product.

A few concrete shifts to make in the rewrite:

If the dying ad opened with a product shot, the new version opens with a problem moment. A frustrated person. A messy scene. A relatable scenario that the viewer instantly recognizes from their own life.

If the dying ad relied on stats and features, the new version relies on visualizing the desire. Show what life looks like after the problem is solved. Make the viewer want it before they know what it costs.

If the dying ad sold the feature, the new version sells the feeling.

This is the difference between an ad that scales for one week and an ad that scales for a year.

A few other things can also kill a winning ad, and these have less to do with your ad and more to do with the moment around it:

  • Seasonal demand shifts. If your ad sold winter coats in March, that's not on you. Demand moved. Same with humidifiers in July, sunscreen in November, you get it.
  • Seasonal visuals. Even if the product is evergreen, a winter background in an ad running in July starts feeling off. The vibe doesn't match the viewer's life anymore, so they scroll past.
  • A competitor launched something better. Sometimes an ad dies because another brand in the same category dropped a stronger creative or a better offer, and the audience's attention shifted there.
  • Audience saturation. If your ad ran hard for a few weeks, every viewer in your target audience might have already seen it five times. They're not buying because they're done with it.

None of these are excuses to skip the rewrite. The rewrite is still the right first move every time. But if the rewrite doesn't bring the ad back to life either, one of these might be the reason. In that case, take the learning, move on, and put your energy into the next bounty.

A winning ad is the start. Not the end.

One winning ad means you've figured out something specific that works for this audience. The hook lands. The angle resonates. The format converts. You don't fully know yet which of those is doing the most work. But you have signal. And signal is worth iterating on hard.

Here's what to do.

Study the winner first. Pull up the ad. Watch it like you've never seen it before. What's actually working? Is it the hook? The visual? The voice? The specific angle? Spend 30 minutes on this before you do anything else. Form 2 to 3 hypotheses about why it's winning.

Then iterate. Hard.

The fastest iteration moves to try first:

  • Same script, new hook. Take the winning body and swap the first 4 seconds with a different hook. Test which hook variant scales further.
  • Expanded problem section. Stretch the problem framing in the script. Let the viewer sit with the pain a beat longer before the product appears.
  • In medias res. Open the ad in the middle of the desired outcome, with the product already solving the problem, and then flash back to tell the story. Pure pattern-interrupt.
  • Common comment as hook. Look at the comments under your winning ad. Find a real objection or reaction. Use it verbatim as the hook of the next variation.
  • Reframe for a different sub-avatar. Your winner spoke to one type of person. Rewrite the same angle for a different sub-segment of the same audience.
  • Format swap. If your winner is a video, try the same concept as a static. If it's a static, try it as a video.

Don't try all of these at once. Try 2 or 3 over the next few weeks. Submit each one as a new bounty under the same brand.

And one more move while you're iterating. Go back to the brand owner.

You did this in Move 4 when you were generating ideas. Do it again now, with the winning ad. Post your dashboard result in the brand-specific Discord channel and ask the brand owner what they think. Ask why they think it's working. Ask which of the iterations you're planning they'd prioritize first.

This move is even more valuable now than it was when you were guessing at angles. Back then you didn't have data. Now you do. Real performance data on their product, on their audience, on the specific angle that's working. And the brand owner is the only person on earth who can look at that data and tell you what it actually means for their business and their next move.

They're going to spot patterns in the data you missed. They're going to suggest iterations you wouldn't have thought of. They're going to share things about their audience that they've learned over years of running ads, and you couldn't possibly know after one winning ad.

This is the move that separates students who hit one winner from students who turn that one winner into 5, 10, 20 winners over the next year. Use it every single time.

Winning ads compound. Each iteration that scales adds to your payout. Each iteration that fails teaches you something specific about the brand's audience. Either way you win.
