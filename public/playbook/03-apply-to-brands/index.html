A Short Sales Manual For Working With Brands

Written by Karlo · 28-05-2026

A few years ago, my Upwork profile had zero earnings on it.

Anyone scanning my profile had every reason to skip me. No completed jobs. No reviews. No social proof. On paper I should have been invisible.

I was still landing $5K and $10K clients every month.

Not because I was a better editor than everyone else applying. Half of them had more polished portfolios than mine. Some had years more experience.

The difference was how I sold myself when I applied.

This article is the short manual on that part. How to position yourself in front of a brand owner so they pick you out of the 30 other applicants going for the same job.

Every move below applies whether you're applying through the EcomTalent Job Board, cold DMing a brand owner you found on X, or sending a proposal on Upwork. The channel changes. The fundamentals don't.

One principle to lock in before we start.

It's About Them. Not You.

Every word of this article flows from one principle.

Your application is about THEM, not you.

A brand owner does not care about you when they first read your message. They don't care about your years of experience. They don't care about your portfolio. They don't care about your story. They don't care that you took this course.

They care about one thing.

Will this person help my brand make more money?

That's the whole filter. Every word of every message you send to a brand is either answering that question or wasting their time.

And here's where almost every student breaks. They lead with themselves.

"Hi, I'm [name]. I'm a video editor. I have 3 years of experience editing ads. Here's my portfolio. Looking forward to hearing back."

That message doesn't answer the brand owner's question. It answers a question they weren't asking. So they close the tab and move on to the next applicant.

The fix is brutal in its simplicity.

Talk about them more than you talk about yourself.

If you count "you" and "your" words in your application and compare them to "I" and "me" and "my" words, the "you/your" count should be way higher. Not slightly higher. Way higher. Like 4 to 1.

When a brand owner opens an application that's about them - their market, their competitors, what could be sharper in their current ads, what you'd test - they feel something almost no other application gives them. They feel like the person on the other end actually thought about their business.

That alone puts you in the top 5% of applicants. Before you've written a single line about yourself.

The Principles Of A Great Application

I'm not going to give you a script. I'm not going to give you a template. I'm not going to tell you to write a specific document or fill out a specific form a specific way. Every brand wants something different. Some have a job board form with set fields. Some want a DM. Some want a Loom. Some just want you to email them a portfolio link.

The format isn't the move. The principles are.

Get these three right and the format takes care of itself.

Principle 1 - Educate yourself on the brand

If the brand is revealed in the job post, you have no excuse.

Spend 30 to 60 minutes on them before you write a single word.

Pull up their website. Look at their product. Look at their pricing. Look at how they position themselves.

Open their TikTok and Instagram. See the content they post organically. Notice what gets engagement and what doesn't.

Pull up Meta Ad Library and look at every ad they're currently running. Notice the angles they keep coming back to. Notice the ones that have been running for weeks (those are winning) and the ones that died fast (those didn't).

If you have access to Atria, run their account through it. Their top-performing ads, their hooks, their formats.

Now you actually know their business. You know what they sell. You know who they sell to. You know what's working and what isn't.

The brand owner feels this in the first 10 seconds of reading your application. Every applicant who didn't do the work sounds generic. You won't.

If the brand is NOT revealed, do the same work for the niche. If the post says "DTC supplement brand targeting women," go look at the top supplement brands targeting women. Form an opinion on what's working in that space and what isn't.

Either way, the homework happens BEFORE you start writing. Don't skip it.

Principle 2 - Understand the opportunity

Read the entire job post. Twice.

Every job post has information most applicants miss because they only skimmed it. The niche. The format they're looking for (UGC vs static vs AI vs VSL). The volume they need. The pay structure. The tone of how the brand owner writes (corporate vs scrappy).

Sometimes brand owners sneak in screening filters. They'll ask you to include a specific word in your reply just to see who actually reads the post. Skip that detail and your application gets thrown out instantly.

Read it twice. Mark what they need. Mark what they're NOT asking for, because those are the things you can pitch as upside in your application.

You can't sell someone on a solution if you don't understand the problem they're trying to solve. Read carefully or don't apply.

Principle 3 - Make every word about them

Once you've done the research, the application writes itself.

You're not writing a resume. You're writing TO the brand owner. Like a conversation.

Talk about their market. Talk about their competition. Talk about the patterns you noticed in their ads. Tell them what you'd test if you got hired. Get specific - actual angles, actual hooks, actual formats. Not "I'd test some new creative." Real ideas.

Then, at the end, briefly - and I mean briefly - talk about yourself. Two or three lines. Who you are. What you do. Why you're the fit. Link to your portfolio.

End with a clear ask. A call. A reply. A next step. Don't end with "thanks, looking forward to hearing back." That's a dead-end. End with "Want to hop on a 30-minute call this week to go deeper?"

A simple test before you send. Read the whole thing out loud. If it sounds like you're talking ABOUT yourself, rewrite it. If it sounds like you're talking TO the brand owner about their business, you're ready to send.

And one rule that applies everywhere. Never use AI to write the application. Brand owners can smell AI copy from the first sentence. You did the research. You have real opinions. Just write them like you'd say them out loud to a friend.

The Loom Move

If you only do one thing from this article, do this.

Remember the zero-earnings Upwork profile from the start of this article?

The reason it worked was the Loom. Every application I sent had one attached. The Loom was the entire difference between getting hired and being ignored.

One of those clients told me directly: "I only reached out to you because of the Loom. Your profile looked shady. The Loom showed me you actually know what you're talking about."

That's the leverage we're working with. A 2-minute video that beats a portfolio you'd spend years building.

If a brand owner asks for a Loom in the job post, you record one. If they don't ask for one, you record one anyway. The Loom is your unfair advantage. Use it every single time you can.

Here are the principles of a great Loom.

Record it so they can tell it's for them. Open the Loom by sharing your screen on their job post. Now the brand owner watching can literally see you scrolling through their job post. Proof that this isn't recycled from another application. That single detail puts you ahead of every Loom you don't see.

Keep it short. 2 to 3 minutes max. Brand owners are busy. A 7-minute Loom doesn't show effort, it shows you can't edit your own thinking. The same skill that goes into making a good ad goes into recording a good Loom. Get to the point.

Lead with them, not you. Spend the first 15 to 20 seconds on yourself. Name. What you do. One line about your background. Then immediately pivot to their brand. Their market. Their competition. What you'd test for them. If the brand is revealed, switch screen share to their website or Meta Ad Library and walk through it live.

End with the ask. "I'd love to hop on a 30-minute call this week to go deeper. Here's my Calendly link. Pick a time that works for you." Don't end with "thanks, looking forward to hearing back." End with the next step.

Then paste the Loom link at the top of your written application. The text application can be three lines:

Hey, I recorded a quick Loom for you - [link]

Here's a link to some of my work so far - [portfolio]

Cheers, [your name]

That's it. The Loom does the rest.

The reason this works is simple. 99% of applicants apply with text. When you send a Loom, you show the brand owner that you read their post, cared enough to record something personalized, and can communicate like a human. The Loom is a 2-minute audition for the call.

If you're new and don't have a portfolio yet, the Loom is your single most valuable asset. It's the closest thing the brand owner has to interviewing you before they ever message you back.

The Move That Gets You Hired

Out of everything in this article, this is the move that gets you hired more than any other.

When a brand is revealed in the job post, drop everything else and do this.

Spend 30 to 60 minutes making them something. Then send them the work BEFORE you sell yourself.

A few examples of what "making them something" looks like.

  • Generate 3 to 5 AI static ads using their product images. Midjourney, Higgsfield, Nano Banana, whatever you use.
  • Pull their top 5 ads from Meta Ad Library and write a 1-page breakdown of what's working and what's not.
  • Build a quick market research doc on their niche. Avatars, pain points, the language the audience actually uses.
  • Find 2 to 3 of their TikTok organic videos and rewrite them as ad concepts you'd test.
  • Record a second Loom walking through 3 specific ad ideas you'd ship for them in week 1.

Don't try to do all five. Pick one. Maybe two. An hour total, max.

Then your application opens like this:

Hey, my name is [name].

I made these quick AI statics for your brand - [link]

Have a look.

Then you say one or two lines about yourself, link your portfolio, and end with the ask. That's the whole application.

The reason this is the #1 move is simple. You're not asking for the job. You're showing them what working with you looks like.

Words can lie. Work can't.

A brand owner who reads your application has to take everything you say on faith. A brand owner who opens your application and sees actual ads you made for their brand doesn't have to take anything on faith. They can see the work. They can judge it. They can decide if they like your taste in 30 seconds.

That's a completely different position to negotiate from.

And here's the part most students underestimate. Even when you don't get the job, the brand owner remembers you. The next time they need someone, or a friend of theirs needs someone, your name is in their head first.

Free value work compounds. Even the misses pay off later.

Do this every single time the brand is revealed.

What Trash Applications Actually Look Like

A few years ago when I was grinding on Upwork, I was struggling. I was applying to 20 or 30 job posts a week and barely getting responses. It made no sense to me.

So I did something weird.

I made a fake Upwork business account. Posted a fake job for a video editor. Sat back and watched the applications come in.

What I saw changed how I applied to everything after that.

Almost every application looked the same. Same template. Same opening line. Same brag list. Same closing. Some were obviously written by AI from the first sentence. Some were so generic they could have been sent to any job post in any industry on any platform.

After that experiment, I rebuilt my whole approach. And the patterns I saw on Upwork 4 years ago are the same patterns I still see in applications today. They never change.

Here's the trash list. If your applications look like any of these, that's why you're not hearing back.

1. The AI cover letter. Polished, vague, says nothing specific. "I am thrilled to apply for this exciting opportunity that aligns perfectly with my passion for performance-driven video content." Dead on arrival. Brand owners spot AI copy in one sentence.

2. The copy-paste template. No mention of the brand. No mention of the niche. No mention of anything specific from THIS job post. The same exact message sent to 50 different jobs. Brand owners see 10 of these a week and skip past them in 3 seconds.

3. The brag list. "I have 5 years of experience. I've worked with X, Y, Z. I'm skilled in After Effects, Premiere, Photoshop, Figma." None of that answers the only question the brand owner is asking. Skipped.

4. The one-line DM. "Hey, I'm a video editor, here's my portfolio." That's the entire message. No effort. No thought. No chance.

5. The novel. The opposite mistake. Three paragraphs about themselves. Their journey into marketing. Their love for creativity. Their family. The brand owner clicks away by paragraph 2.

If your applications look like any of these, that's the gap.

The fix is everything in the principles above. Do the research. Talk about them. Send a Loom. Drop in some free value work.

That's the whole shift.

Apply To Everything

Even when you do every principle perfectly, you won't land every brand you apply to.

That's normal. Hiring is unpredictable. Some brand owners are slow. Some already have someone in mind. Some take 3 weeks to make a decision.

The fix isn't to make your applications even more perfect. The fix is to apply more.

Go into the Job Board channel on Discord. Scroll through every job post. Apply to all of them.

Yes, all of them.

You'll naturally have higher chances of landing the most recent posts because those brand owners are hiring right now. They're actively reviewing applications this week. They want to fill the role before the month is over.

But don't skip the older posts. Some brand owners take weeks to make a decision. Some already interviewed people and didn't find a fit. Some are slowly reviewing applications between meetings. A post from 10 days ago is very much still open. You never know which old post turns into your next $5K retainer. Apply to it anyway.

A few things to keep in mind while you're putting in the volume.

Don't get discouraged when you don't hear back immediately. Average response time when I was applying heavily was 2 to 3 days. Some brand owners took 2 weeks. Don't sit there refreshing your inbox. Send the application and move on to the next one.

Track what's working. After 20 to 30 applications, you'll start to see patterns. Which type of brand responds. Which Loom angle gets the best reception. Which CTA gets the most calls booked. Adjust based on data, not gut.

Don't lower the bar to keep your pace up. A bad application sent fast is worse than a great application sent slow. Spend 30 to 60 minutes on each one. Treat every application like the brand could pay you $5K a month, because they might.

The students who hit consistent retainers off the Job Board aren't the ones with the best portfolios. They're the ones who applied to 40 posts when most students applied to 4.

If You're Brand New

You might be reading this thinking "I don't have any results yet. This article doesn't apply to me."

It applies more to you than anyone else.

Being a beginner is an advantage if you frame it right. Brand owners aren't only looking for experience. They're looking for someone hungry. Someone who'll outwork the experienced person who's coasting on a resume.

And you're not coming in empty. You finished the 30-day sprint. You did the action items. You have ads you made for the briefs. You have drafts, scripts, statics, edits sitting on your hard drive. That's proof you can do the work. You just don't have a winning client ad yet.

The frame to lead with when you apply is honesty.

I just finished the 30-day sprint inside EcomTalent.

I don't have a winning client ad on my portfolio yet.

But I'm dead serious about getting reps in.

I'd love to work with you at a reduced rate, or even for free in exchange for experience and a testimonial.

When you offer that, you take all the risk off the brand. They get someone hungry who'll grind. You get the experience and your first case study.

Your goal at this stage is not money.

Your goal is experience.

The first brand. The first dashboard with your name on it. The first real case study. The first testimonial. Once you have 2 or 3 of those, you've left the beginner stage behind. Now you have experience. Now you have results. Now you have a portfolio. Now you can charge.

Money becomes the byproduct. Experience is the asset.

Don't waste the beginner stage holding out for a rate that matches your ego. Get the reps. The rest follows.
